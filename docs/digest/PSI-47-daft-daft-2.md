# PSI-47: DAFT / DAFT-2 (Dust and Aerosol Measurement Feasibility Test / Dust and Aerosol Measurement Feasibility Test-2)

Before flying a smoke-measuring experiment, NASA checked on the Space Station that a commercial particle counter, which normally relies on gravity to recycle its alcohol, still counted particles correctly in weightlessness, and in doing so made the first measurement of how dusty the Station air is.

## Question

Can a modified commercial condensation nuclei counter (the TSI P-Trak) count aerosol particles reliably in long-duration microgravity? It was to be the zeroth-moment (number concentration) instrument of the SAME smoke experiment. The P-Trak grows particles into countable droplets by condensing isopropanol on them, and its sloped condenser relies on gravity to drain excess alcohol back to the wick. Two failure modes were feared. Liquid alcohol might clog or obscure the nozzle, laser and optics. The internal flow in the optics chamber might over-count particles. The hypotheses were that the capillary-modified P-Trak would function normally in microgravity, and that the test aerosol, made and filtered as designed, would be within both instruments' ranges. The objective was to retire this risk to SAME by comparing the P-Trak against a DustTrak (insensitive to gravity) and a reference dust aerosol.

## Summary

DAFT was a quick risk-reduction test for the Smoke Aerosol Measurement Experiment (SAME). SAME depended on a TSI P-Trak condensation particle counter, which uses gravity to return condensed isopropanol to its wick. NASA cut capillary grooves into the condenser and flew it with a gravity-insensitive TSI DustTrak photometer as reference. In February and March 2005 (Increment 10) the crew sampled cabin air at three locations and air dusted by separating Velcro. In August 2006 (Increment 13) three 15 L bags of Arizona Road Dust in nitrogen were sampled. The P-Trak ran with no clogging or errors. It read up to about 45,000 particles/cm3 for Velcro dust and 11,400-36,500 particles/cm3 (DustTrak 33-71 mg/m3) for the bags. Undisturbed ISS air averaged under 15 particles/cm3 and under 0.005 mg/m3, far cleaner than the Shuttle or offices. The orbital mass-per-count ratio was about 3.2 times the ground value (mean diameter 1.65 µm), consistent with large particles not settling. The modified counter was accepted for SAME, supporting spacecraft smoke-detector design.

## Physics

Smoke detectors respond to particle size. Ionisation detectors favour small particles; scattering detectors favour particles near and above the wavelength. Spacecraft detectors were designed from 1-g smoke data, but shuttle experiments suggested smoke particles are larger in microgravity, so SAME set out to measure moments of the smoke size distribution in orbit. A condensation particle counter makes particles as small as about 20 nm visible by drawing them through a warm, alcohol-saturated region and then a cooled condenser. The vapour becomes supersaturated and condenses on each particle, growing droplets of a few micrometres that are counted one by one with a laser. On Earth, excess liquid draining down the sloped condenser returns to the wick by gravity. Without gravity it could pool and flood the optics, so the condenser was given fine longitudinal grooves that move liquid by capillary (surface-tension) forces instead. A light-scattering photometer (DustTrak) measures mass concentration and does not depend on gravity. Dividing its mass reading by the number count gives an average particle mass, hence an equivalent diameter, which is a check on the counter. In microgravity, particles in a sample bag do not settle, so a bag aerosol keeps more of its large particles than on Earth.

$$
\bar{m}_p = \frac{C_m}{N}, \qquad \bar{D} = \left(\frac{6\,\bar{m}_p}{\pi\,\rho_p}\right)^{1/3}
$$

Average particle mass and equivalent diameter from the two instruments, as computed in the DAFT spreadsheets. $C_m$ is the DustTrak mass concentration (convert mg/m3 to g/cm3 by multiplying by 1e-9); $N$ is the P-Trak number concentration (particles/cm3); $\bar{m}_p$ is the mean mass per particle (g); $\rho_p$ is the assumed particle density (taken as 1 g/cm3, water); $\bar{D}$ is the diameter of average mass (cm). For DAFT-3 bag 1 (voice-down averages 43.15 mg/m3 and 17,314 cm-3) this gives $\bar{m}_p$ = 2.49e-12 g and $\bar{D}$ = 1.68 µm. Valid only as a consistency check. The DustTrak is calibrated for a reference dust, the P-Trak undercounts particles above its range, and the true density of Arizona Road Dust is not 1 g/cm3.

$$
d^{*} = \frac{4\,\sigma\,v_m}{k_B\,T\,\ln S}
$$

Kelvin equation: the smallest particle diameter $d^*$ (m) on which vapour condenses and grows at saturation ratio $S$. $\sigma$ is the liquid surface tension (N/m, isopropanol); $v_m$ is the molecular volume of the liquid (m3); $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant (J/K); $T$ is temperature (K). The P-Trak's cooled condenser sets $S$ so that particles down to about 0.02 µm are activated, which fixes its lower size limit. The equation is unaffected by gravity, but liquid management in the condenser is not. Applies to insoluble, wettable particles.

Textbook background: Kelvin equation for heterogeneous condensation in condensation particle counters.

## Method

### Apparatus

The P-Trak is a handheld condensation nuclei counter for particles 0.02 to more than 1 µm, with a maximum count of 500,000 particles/cm3. Its pump draws sample through a saturator, where an alcohol-soaked wick supplies isopropanol vapour, then through a cooled condenser tube where droplets grow, and past a laser and photodetector. The flight unit's condenser had about 60 machined longitudinal grooves to return liquid alcohol to the evaporator by capillary action. Its mercury orientation switch was removed, and it was flight-hardened and covered in foil tape for flammability. The DustTrak, a laser (Mie) scattering photometer reading mass concentration in mg/m3 for 0.1-10 µm particles, was the reference; it was fitted with a 2.5 µm inlet impactor for the dust tests. DAFT-1 contained the modified P-Trak, alcohol wick and batteries; DAFT-2 the DustTrak and batteries. DAFT-3 was the aerosol system: a fill assembly that metered ISS gaseous nitrogen through a dust holder (0.25 g Arizona Road Dust) into 15 L Mylar bags, and a DAFT impactor to strip particles above about 2.5 µm (about 50% efficient). DAFT-4 was an unmodified, flight-hardened P-Trak. HEPA filters zeroed both instruments before and after each run.

### Fuels and samples

- ISS cabin air (undisturbed), US Lab at EXPRESS Rack 4, Lab 1 near Port rack 6, and Node 1 (aerosol, ambient air drawn through a common sampling hose)
- Lint/dust generated by repeatedly separating Velcro at the sampling inlet (aerosol, ad hoc high-concentration aerosol)
- Arizona Road Dust (ARD, Fine) dispersed in ISS gaseous nitrogen, 0.25 g per dust holder (about 25 mg reaching each bag) (aerosol, polydisperse reference dust in 15 L Mylar bag, impactor-filtered)

### Procedure

DAFT-1 and -2 reached the ISS on Progress 16P in December 2004. With no dust source yet on board, cabin air was sampled. Session 1 was on 22 Feb 2005 (GMT 053, Leroy Chiao, Increment 10), test point 1 at EXPRESS Rack 4, about 12 min. Session 2 was on 4 Mar 2005 (GMT 063) with three test points of about 9-13 min each: Velcro separation at the inlet, Lab 1 near Port rack 6, and Node 1 mid-cabin. Each run was: battery in, warm-up (P-Trak about 1 min), HEPA zero check (P-Trak below 10 particles/cm3, DustTrak -0.001 to 0.003 mg/m3), about 6 min of sampling through a shared hose, a second HEPA zero, then shutdown. Both units logged at 1 s, and video was used to synchronise clocks. In Increment 13 (Jeff Williams), the first ARD session (GMT 229, 2006) was aborted. The fill assembly's HEPA filter was found broken off and a laptop COM-port mismatch blocked downloads, so a cabin-air sample was taken instead. After a safety review the opening was covered with grey tape. On 21 Aug 2006 (GMT 233) three bags were filled with 4, 6 and 8 toggles of the dust fill valve and sampled through the DAFT impactor. Readings were called down every 30 s and recorded on the ground, because the electronic data from these runs did not arrive by downlink. DAFT-4 (unmodified P-Trak) was not run. Only 3 of up to 15 planned dust test points were made.

## Fire-safety relevance

DAFT burned nothing. Its fire-safety value is in detection. It validated in orbit the condensation particle counter that SAME used to measure the number concentration of smoke from spacecraft materials, data needed to redesign smoke detectors that were sized from 1-g smoke. It also measured the ISS background particle level a detector must discriminate against: under 15 particles/cm3 and 0.005 mg/m3 in undisturbed air, with transient spikes above 30,000 particles/cm3 from ordinary crew activity (Velcro). The low background helps detection sensitivity, but nuisance sources can produce large short-lived signals. The finding that bag aerosols kept more large particles in microgravity illustrates why particle sizes differ from 1 g. Caveats: dust, not smoke, was sampled; only one counter unit; few test points; no comparison with the unmodified instrument.

### Habitat conditions

All sampling was in normal ISS cabin air, essentially the Earth sea-level atmosphere (20.9% O2, 101.3 kPa); O2 and pressure were not recorded by DAFT. No measurement was made at reduced pressure or enriched O2 such as the proposed Moon-habitat atmosphere (30% O2 or more at 70.3 kPa or less). Condensation-counter performance and particle behaviour at lower pressure were therefore not tested. Ambient pressure affects the instrument's flow and vapour supersaturation, but this was not evaluated.

### Earth benefits

The investigators' report states there are no tangible Earth benefits from DAFT. The PSI record suggests the microgravity-adapted counter could inform instruments for other difficult environments such as submarines or underwater laboratories.

## Limitations

- Reference aerosol was polydisperse Arizona Road Dust, not the intended monodisperse aerosol (about 0.7 µm, about 50,000 particles/cm3). Its size distribution in the bag was not measured in orbit, so the P-Trak's absolute counting accuracy could only be inferred from a consistency check.
- Only three dust test points, one instrument unit, and no unmodified-P-Trak control (DAFT-4 not run). The P-Trak bag averages (about 17,000-27,000 particles/cm3) were below the 30,000-100,000 range the team considered optimal, although the DustTrak readings (33-71 mg/m3) were within its optimal 20-80 mg/m3.
- The 2005 cabin-air readings never exceeded 20% of either instrument's range, which the team itself called 'somewhat suspect' as a performance test.
- The alcohol supply was old (wick loaded over 3 years earlier), and particle build-up in the optics may have biased the bag 3 counts low.
- The derived particle diameters assume unit density and a fixed DustTrak calibration. They are diameters of average mass, not measured size distributions.

## Open questions

- Re-analyse the 1 s logged time series in the Retrieved Data Summary for all seven runs (rather than 30 s voice-down values). Quantify the count/mass ratio drift during each bag, to separate particle build-up or low alcohol from aerosol evolution in the bag.
- Recompute equivalent diameters with the true ARD density (about 2.65 g/cm3) and the DustTrak's ARD calibration, and compare with the ARD Fine size distribution after the impactor.
- Use the 2005 cabin-air logs (with video timing) as a background-noise reference for spacecraft smoke-detector false-alarm studies, alongside later ISS particulate surveys.
- Compare DAFT background levels with the SAME and SAME-R (PSI-102, PSI-101) background measurements taken with the same instrument type.

## Gaps and data-quality notes

- The experimental table is misleading. Its two rows are labelled as sessions D1 (GMT 54, 2005) and D2 (GMT 63, 2005) but describe hardware sets DAFT-1 (modified P-Trak) and DAFT-2 (DustTrak); both sessions used both instruments. Both rows say Arizona Road Dust was sampled, but the 2005 sessions sampled cabin air and Velcro debris. ARD was used only on 21 Aug 2006 (DAFT-3), which the table omits, as it omits the aborted 17 Aug 2006 session. tests.csv inherits this: 2 'condition_set' rows with fuel 'arizona road dust' and date 2005-02-23.
- Date inconsistencies. GMT 54 in the table corresponds to 23 Feb 2005, but the reports, analysed-data file name (GMT053) and instrument logs give 22 Feb 2005. The 30-day report says the first 2006 session was 'August 18, 2006 (GMT 229)', but GMT 229 of 2006 is 17 Aug, matching the instrument log date. The time-history workbook has a garbled GMT entry '7:181:25' for bag 1 at 360 s. Bag 3 times are offset by one row between the 'time hist' and 'urban' workbooks.
- The final report (.doc), 1-year report (.doc) and two .ppt decks have no extracted text in evidence.parquet. They were read by extracting text runs directly from the binary files and are cited as psi_table with sheet 'Word document body text' / 'slide text'. This is a workaround: these sources cannot be checked by the quote validator. The 1-year report is an earlier draft of the final report with near-identical content. The condenser-modification drawing (PDF, no text layer) was not read.
- The 30-day report PDF text layer is column-scrambled (fragments missing first letters, for example 'avid Urban'). Only clean spans were quoted; figures 1-7 (time traces and ground correlation) exist only as images.
- Eight Analyzed Data workbooks ('flight data 063' and its _DTrk/_PTrk/_TIMING variants) contain the same sheets and are redundant. Raw Data has text logs only for 2005 test IDs 2-4. Test ID 1 and the 2006 logs exist only inside the Excel workbooks.
- The record's 'removed' list holds 185 legacy files (about 5 GB), 178 of them under 'Legacy/DAFT P-TRAK Experiment Data/DAFT/CVB/...' (bitmap images named stability_*.bmp). These look like misfiled data from another investigation (CVB, possibly the Constrained Vapor Bubble), not DAFT. They were not downloaded.
- Source inconsistency: NTRS 20050215648 says the DAFT impactor removes the smallest particles, whereas the DAFT reports say it removes the larger ones (above about 2.5 µm). The reports were followed.
- The fact sheet lists the particle size range in '(mm)'; values 0.02 to >1.0 and 0.10 to 10 are micrometres.
- Investigator roles differ by source. PSI lists Urban (PI) and Fortenberry (Project Scientist); the fact sheet and final report list Urban and Ruff as PIs; the 30-day report lists Co-Is Mulholland, Yuan, Yang and Cleary. The record gives the acronym 'DAFT / DAFT-2', but DAFT-1 to -4 were hardware packages of one experiment.
- Personal data: the fact sheet, NTRS 20050215648 and the 2007 presentation contain phone numbers and e-mail addresses; the time-history workbook names the ground staff who transcribed data. These were not copied beyond the verbatim measurement-provenance quote. No text addressed to an AI was found.
- External sources used: NTRS 20050215648 (2004 Research & Technology article), NTRS 20070031686 (2007 ISDC presentation). The two DOI publications (AIAA 2005-341, SAE 2005-01-2930) were not read; their content overlaps the PSI presentation 'SmokeDetectionfromMicrogravityFires.ppt'. The DAFT web page cited in the documents (microgravity.grc.nasa.gov/combustion/daft/) was not tried, as it is a defunct GRC address.
- Update to gaps[10] (publication review, 2026-11-15): the abstracts of both DOI publications were read via OpenAlex. SAE 2005-01-2930 confirms findings[1] (first ISS ambient-aerosol measurement, very low levels); AIAA 2005-341 is a programmatic research plan that does not mention DAFT. Full texts are paywalled.
