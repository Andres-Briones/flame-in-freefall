# PSI-25: BASS-II (Burning and Suppression of Solids - II)

Astronauts ran 129 burns of small plastic and fabric samples in a gentle, adjustable air stream on the ISS while lowering the oxygen with nitrogen, to find the weakest airflow and the lowest oxygen at which solids keep burning without gravity, and to compare that with the Earth test NASA uses to approve materials.

## Question

BASS-II tested the same hypothesis as BASS (PSI-26): a material in microgravity with adequate ventilation burns as well as, or better than, the same material in normal gravity at the same pressure, O2 concentration and temperature. NASA screens spacecraft materials with an upward-burning 1 g test (NASA-STD-6001 Test 1), considered the worst case on Earth, but it may not be conservative in space. BASS-II set out to (1) study ignition, flame growth, flame spread and extinction limits of solid fuels in low-velocity forced flows; (2) bridge the gap between Test 1, ground-based microgravity tests and real flammability in microgravity; (3) give preliminary data to refine the science requirements of the follow-on SoFIE project; and (4) use practical, thicker fuels in realistic geometries (slabs, rods, a spherical section, films and fabrics). Many tests looked for the minimum O2 or minimum flow at which a material still burns in space, to compare with Earth-based limits. Others looked at how fast a fire grows and whether the flame reaches a finite size, which matters for firefighting strategy. The new variable compared with BASS was ambient O2, lowered below 21% by adding nitrogen to the glovebox. Despite the project's name, the PSI documents describe no suppression tests in BASS-II; the nitrogen was used only to dilute the atmosphere.

## Summary

BASS-II ran 129 burns of small solid samples (126 in 2014, three in 2016) in a 7.6 cm square flow duct in the ISS Microgravity Science Glovebox. Imposed air flows ranged from below 1 to about 55 cm/s. For the first time on ISS the glovebox was diluted with nitrogen, so oxygen ranged from 22.2% to 13.6%. Five teams studied separate configurations: thin PMMA films and thick PMMA slabs in opposed flow, PMMA rods in opposed and concurrent flow, and a cotton-fibreglass fabric in concurrent flow. Thin-film flames went out from radiative loss at about 1 cm/s in air, whatever their thickness, and at 2.6-4 cm/s near 16% O2. The limit fitted for concurrent rod flames has its minimum at about 13.5% O2 (lowest reading with a flame: 13.6%), against a 15.4% upward-burning limit on Earth. Opposed spread over rods persisted to 17.5% O2, against 19% in normal gravity. Fabric flames quenched at 1-5 cm/s. Most flames released under 100 W and burned fuel-rich, producing much CO. Weak ventilation near spacecraft surfaces can therefore sustain flames where Earth tests show none.

## Physics

A flame spreading over a solid heats the unburnt fuel ahead of it to its pyrolysis temperature. The fuel vapour then mixes with oxygen and burns. In opposed flow (air moving against the spread direction, like downward burning on Earth) the heating comes from a small region at the flame's leading edge. In concurrent flow (air moving with the spread, like upward burning) the whole flame preheats the fuel and can grow. The gas residence time at the leading edge scales as $\alpha_g/V_g^2$. Three regimes follow. At high flow, finite-rate chemistry cannot keep up with the short residence time and the flame blows off (kinetic regime; a small Damkohler number). At moderate flow, the spread rate of a thin fuel is set only by heat conduction and is independent of flow (thermal regime of de Ris). At very low flow, the long residence time lets surface and gas radiation remove a growing fraction of the heat, and the flame quenches (radiative regime). Plotted as O2 against flow, these limits form a U-shaped flammability boundary with a blowoff branch and a quenching branch. On Earth, buoyancy imposes its own flow at the flame: about 0.5 m/s at ignition for the BASS-II rods, and of order 20 cm/s at the base of an upward flame. So the low-flow quenching branch and the bottom of the U, where the minimum oxygen for burning lies, can only be reached in sustained microgravity. For rods and stagnation flames the investigators use a stretch rate $a$ in place of velocity. At about 1-2 cm/s, comparable to a diffusive velocity of 2 cm/s, convection and diffusion supply oxygen at similar rates, and the flame changes from residence-time-limited to diffusion-limited. Thermally thin fuels heat through their whole thickness, so spread rate varies as 1/thickness. Thick fuels such as millimetre PMMA slabs keep a cold core, so ignition and growth are slower and heat loss into the solid and the holder matters.

$$
V_{f,\mathrm{thin}} = \frac{\pi}{4}\,\frac{\lambda_g F}{\rho_s c_s \tau}, \qquad F = \frac{T_f - T_v}{T_v - T_\infty}
$$

de Ris thermal-regime spread rate, thin fuel. Opposed-flow spread rate $V_f$ (m/s) over a thermally thin fuel of half-thickness $\tau$ (m), density $\rho_s$ (kg/m3) and specific heat $c_s$ (kJ/kg K); $\lambda_g$ is gas conductivity at $T_v$ (kW/m K); $F$ is the de Ris flame coefficient with flame, vaporisation and ambient temperatures $T_f$, $T_v$, $T_\infty$ (K). Valid in the thermal regime, i.e. flow neither so high that kinetics matter nor so low that radiation matters; independent of flow speed. Bhattacharjee fitted $F = 4.76$ for PMMA in 21% O2 from the ISS maximum spread rates (2.2 mm/s at 100 um), close to the value from the adiabatic flame temperature.

## Method

### Apparatus

The BASS hardware from PSI-26 was reused with new samples, igniters and flash cards. The flow duct was the 7.6 x 7.6 x 17 cm black-anodised duct originally built for SPICE gas-jet flames, installed in the MSG in the ISS Destiny Lab. A variable-speed fan drove air through a honeycomb straightener and an inlet screen. Perforated restrictor plates (0, 1 or 2) on the fan inlet set the range: up to about 55 cm/s with none and about 35 cm/s with two. A TSI 8475 omnidirectional air-velocity transducer measured duct flow. It had a response time of about 1 min and displayed whole cm/s only (about +/-0.5 cm/s). So low flows were set by fan potentiometer and converted using fan calibrations made at the end of each operations day. The fan stops at a finite voltage (above 3.8 V), so the lowest flows sit just above shut-off. An uncalibrated thermopile radiometer (CaF2 window, 0.13-11 um) in the downstream top corner gave a relative flame signal. Samples were lit with Kanthal A-1 29-gauge hot-wire igniters (0.8-1 ohm, 3.5-3.8 A). A Nikon D300s still camera (4,320 x 2,968 pixels, 60 mm lens) looked through the top window at about 1 frame/s. A colour video camera looked through the front window via a mirror; its overlay showed N2 flow, fan voltage, air speed and radiometer. New in BASS-II: the MSG GN2 supply (up to 0.5 L/min through a mass-flow controller) diluted the 250 L glovebox work volume. Before and after each burn, hand-held CSA-CP (O2 at 0.1% resolution, CO 0-1,000 ppm, plus HCl and HCN) and CDM (CO2 0-5%) sensors read the well-mixed work-volume gas. The CSA-CP O2 reading was corrected each day by a constant offset to the ISS Major Constituents Analyzer (MCA). Combustion products left the duct into the sealed work volume, whose filters convert CO to CO2 during the post-test purge.

### Fuels and samples

- Bhattacharjee: thin cast PMMA films, 100, 200, 300 and 400 um thick, 2 cm wide (and 1 cm wide at 200 um), about 9.5-10 cm long, in stainless frames with integral igniters; 22 test points (solid, thin flat film, burned on both faces, opposed flow)
- Miller and Wichman: thick clear cast PMMA slabs, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 mm thick, 1.2 or 2.2 cm wide, 10 cm long; one-sided (backed by a metal holder) or two-sided burning; 20 samples (solid, thick flat slab, opposed flow)
- Olson: cast PMMA rods with initially hemispherical tips, radius 0.318 cm (1/4 in diameter, most tests), 0.476 cm (3/8 in) and 0.635 cm (1/2 in), mostly clear; two black 1/4 in rods for comparison (solid, rod ignited at upstream tip (axial stagnation / concurrent flow))
- Fernandez-Pello and Link: cast PMMA rods 6.35, 9.525 and 12.7 mm diameter, 57.15 mm long, hemispherical ends; mostly black PMMA (as used in the MIST ground project, to limit in-depth radiation absorption), a few clear (solid, rod ignited at downstream end (opposed-flow spread))
- T'ien: SIBAL (Solid Inflammability Boundary at Low Speeds) fabric, 75% cotton / 25% fibreglass, about 0.32 mm thick, 18.2 mg/cm2, 1.2 and 2.2 cm wide, 10 cm exposed length (solid, thin flat fabric sheet in stainless holder, concurrent flow)
- T'ien: one 4-cm-diameter PMMA spherical section (forward-stagnation ignition and flame growth on a thick body); 2 runs (solid, spherical section, stagnation flow)
- Ferkul: Nomex III ('Nomex natural') aramid fabric, 2 cm wide, 3 samples (Nomex is used aboard ISS as a fire barrier); plus one leftover BASS Japan-wax candle (solid, flat fabric in concurrent flow; candle wick in opposed flow)

### Procedure

Each PI had a priority-ordered matrix of about 20 samples, and the ground team directed the crew in real time. For reduced-O2 tests, the crew first flowed GN2 into the closed work volume (MSG air circulation off, BASS fan at maximum) for 25-225 min, as timed by the CSTR dilution model. They recorded CSA-CP and CDM readings before and after each burn. The crew mounted a sample, positioned the igniter within about 1 mm of it, set the flow restrictor and fan, and pulled the igniter lever until a flame appeared; ignition could take up to 60 s in the plan and up to 230 s in practice for rods at low O2. The still camera then ran at about 1 frame/s and the radiometer gain was set. Procedures then differed by sub-investigation. (1) Bhattacharjee: opposed-flow films were ignited at a set flow (mostly 5 cm/s, some 2-10). Flow was then stepped down gradually to radiative extinction, or raised to look for blowoff. Most runs were in air or at about 17% O2, with one at 15%. (2) Miller: thick slabs were burned in opposed flow at 3-20 cm/s for long times, up to about 31 min. Flow was held at plateaus for steady spread and reduced to quenching. O2 was not controlled during a test and drifted down by up to about 3 points as the slab burned. (3) Olson: rods were ignited at the upstream hemispherical tip, typically at 5 cm/s. For quenching limits, the flow was lowered in steps, each held at least about 100 s (the solid response time); tests lasted up to 28 min. For blowoff limits, the flow was raised quickly. Complementary 5.18 s drop-tower blowoff tests were run on the ground. (4) Fernandez-Pello: rods were ignited at the downstream end at high opposed flow. The flow was then reduced in steps, with pauses for steady spread, until extinction. Some tests alternated the fan potentiometer between two settings at 1-2 min intervals to probe the flame's response time. (5) T'ien: fabric was ignited at the leading edge for concurrent spread at 4-11 cm/s, and the flow was often reduced to quenching or raised to blowoff. O2 was fixed by the prior dilution for each test. Partly burned samples were sometimes re-ignited. (6) Ferkul: Nomex was tried in concurrent flow at 8-15 cm/s, and the candle was burned in opposed flow at decreasing flows. After each burn the crew photographed the sample. The work volume was purged for 3 min before access, with further cooling time for touch temperature. The fan was calibrated at the end of each day. Samples were returned to Earth for mass-loss measurements.

## Findings

1. Bhattacharjee, thin PMMA films in opposed flow at about 21% O2: flames went out by radiative extinction as the flow was lowered. This happened at about 1 cm/s for 100, 200 and 300 um films and about 1.5 cm/s for 400 um (1-2 cm wide, about 1 atm). Within the large uncertainty of flow calibration at such low speeds, the extinction velocity does not depend on thickness, although the spread rate does. A 2-D model gave 1.5 cm/s for all four thicknesses. Approaching extinction, the flame shrank and turned from orange to all blue within about 3 s. With the flow cut off completely, a 100 um flame spreading at 2.2 mm/s went out within 2 s. *(limit, high)*

## Fire-safety relevance

Prevention and material screening: BASS-II gives direct evidence that, at about 1 atm, microgravity with weak ventilation can be more hazardous than the 1 g screening configuration. The limit fitted for concurrent PMMA rod flames has its minimum at about 13.5% O2 (lowest reading with a flame: 13.6%), against a 15.4% upward limit in 1 g. Opposed spread over rods persisted to 17.5% O2, against 19% in 1 g, and was faster. Ignition at near-limit O2 took up to about 230 s, against the roughly 25 s igniter time of NASA-STD-6001 Test 1, so a material that 'passes' by not igniting may still burn once lit. The dangerous range of flows (about 1-10 cm/s, around the minimum of the U-shaped boundary) is, in the authors' words, typical of spacecraft ventilation near surfaces. Two practical routes to Earth-based derating come out of the work: extrapolating the linear 1 g blowoff boundary to zero stretch (Olson), and using the de Ris thermal-regime correlation validated in microgravity (Bhattacharjee). For response: in these tests, low-flow flames were extinguished by cutting the ventilation, but hot thermoplastics keep releasing vapour and can eject burning droplets, so suppression should continue until the material has cooled. Nomex III did not sustain a flame in air, which supports its use as a fire barrier. Atmosphere: small (under 100 W), fuel-rich microgravity flames produced CO/CO2 ratios an order of magnitude above efficient combustion. Over 1,000 ppm CO built up in the 250 L glovebox, though dilution into a large module would keep CO low. Limits on transferability: all results are for PMMA, a cotton-fibreglass fabric and Nomex, in a small duct at about 1 atm and 13.6-22.2% O2. They are configuration-specific limits, not certified material ratings.

### Habitat conditions

BASS-II ran at ISS cabin pressure: about 1 atm, with 99.2-99.5 kPa reported for the film tests. O2 ranged from 22.2% down to 13.6% (calibrated readings), close to the Earth sea-level reference (20.9% O2 at 101.3 kPa) and below it. It therefore covers normoxic and hypoxic atmospheres at sea-level pressure only. It does not reach the proposed Moon-habitat atmosphere (30% O2 or more at 70.3 kPa or less) or the 100% O2 / 29.6 kPa suit atmosphere. In those, the O2 partial pressure and mole fraction are higher, and the lower density changes residence times and the buoyant and diffusive velocities. The BASS-II limits (e.g. 13.5% O2 minimum for rods, 1-5 cm/s quenching speeds for fabric) are thus lower-bound reference points; they are not limits for exploration atmospheres. The investigators themselves list low pressure and elevated O2 among expected spacecraft conditions, but did not test them. The flow range (below 1 to about 55 cm/s) spans spacecraft ventilation speeds and near-quiescent pockets.

### Earth benefits

Buoyancy-free data with simple flow fields validate combustion models: the 2-D opposed-spread model, the de Ris correlation, the 3-D transient concurrent-spread model and FDS. These models can then be extended to buoyant flames on Earth, such as wildfires, building fires and waste-to-energy combustion. The microgravity film data showed that downward spread over PMMA in air on Earth is thermally controlled. They also gave a de Ris coefficient and a thin/thick transition thickness of about 5.2 mm usable in terrestrial fire modelling. The work also feeds normal-gravity surrogate test methods: the narrow-channel apparatus (Miller) and the zero-stretch blowoff extrapolation (Olson).

## Limitations

- O2 was measured only before and after each burn, as a work-volume average from a hand-held CSA-CP that was out of calibration and corrected by a daily constant offset to the ISS MCA (0.1% resolution). O2 fell during long burns, by up to 3.2 percentage points (Miller M5), so the O2 at a given flow or at extinction is interpolated (Miller assumed a linear decay). Olson's boundary uses the final reading.
- Flow is poorly known exactly where the limits lie. The air-velocity transducer responds in about 1 min and reads only whole cm/s. The lowest flows were set by fan potentiometer near the fan's shut-off voltage, and in the logs they are recorded as settings (e.g. pot 0.30-0.36) rather than speeds. Daily calibrations were sometimes incomplete, and flow takes time to reach the flame after a change. Bhattacharjee warns of large error bars, and Olson notes the uncertainty below 1 cm/s on the quenching branch.
- Few tests per condition and repeated use of samples. Each PI had about 20 samples spread over several variables, for example only two film tests at about 16% O2, with different widths. Many samples were re-ignited, cut or had their igniter leads removed, which changes geometry and pre-heating (one rod re-burn quenched at a markedly lower flow than the first burn). Ignition was manual, with variable igniter placement and timing.
- Small-duct and holder effects. Heat loss to the sample holders curved the flames and weakened one-sided slab flames. Narrow samples extinguished at higher O2. The duct flow profile was irregular, and wall and fuel boundary layers alter the flow the flame sees. Sample lengths of about 10 cm were too short to fine-tune flow before burnout for concurrent fabric flames, and too short for steady concurrent spread at higher flows.
- The bottom (minimum-O2) part of the fabric flammability boundary could not be found by the varying-flow procedure, because ignition becomes sporadic near the limit; a slowly decreasing-O2 procedure would be needed. Likewise, the rod ignition limit (about 14.5% O2) masks part of the extinction boundary.
- Only PMMA, one cotton-fibreglass fabric, Nomex and a candle were tested, at about 1 atm. The Fernandez-Pello comparison with 1 g used non-overlapping O2 and diameter ranges and buoyant flows an order of magnitude larger, and his clear-rod data were too few to report. No suppression tests were run despite the project name.
- Hardware anomalies: on GMT 051 a very long thick-slab burn overheated and bubbled the Lexan door at a duct leak (repaired on GMT 86/91). Video dropped out after GMT 167 (cable repaired on GMT 170). A flat-sample blowoff attempt on GMT 205 left a soot spot. A camera-card limit cut short one Miller run, and loss of signal interrupted others.

## Open questions

- Convert every logged fan-potentiometer setting at extinction to a flow speed using the daily fan-calibration workbook. Combined with the calibrated final O2, this gives a single extinction-boundary map across all six sub-investigations (films, slabs, two rod configurations, fabric), plotted against stretch or U/U_D.
- Reduce the three 2016 Olson rod runs (O20-O22, flows raised to blowoff at 15-16.5% O2), which are not in the 2021 TM, and add them to the rod blowoff branch.
- Analyse the Fernandez-Pello clear-rod runs (P3a/b, P25) and compare them with black rods, to test the effect of in-depth radiation absorption on opposed spread.
- Tabulate the CSA-CP HCl readings noted in the as-run observations (e.g. up to 12.3 in a low-O2 rod test, units not stated). Check whether they correlate with burn duration or fuel, since PMMA contains no chlorine; the operations procedure blames Freon in the cabin air.
- Measure spread rate and flame length from the still sequences for the Miller slabs at each flow plateau, using within-test O2 interpolation. Then compare with narrow-channel-apparatus ground data to test it as a microgravity surrogate.
- Use the per-test O2/CO2/CO table to recompute heat release rate and CO yield per test, and check the TM's geometry clustering (concurrent rods vs opposed rods vs slabs).

## Gaps and data-quality notes

- No reduced numerical data files are in PSI (spread-rate time series, extinction flow speeds, regression rates). Quantitative results come from the 2021 TM text, its tables (notably Table 5.1, Table 2.1, Table 7.1 and Appendix A) and figure captions. Numbers read only from TM figures (e.g. Fernandez-Pello spread-rate values, T'ien Figure 7.4 spread rates) were not extracted. The TM points to http://flame.sdsu.edu for the Bhattacharjee image data; this was not consulted.
- Imagery is not downloaded: 296 MP4s and 264 zips (274 GB). Only the 125 proof-sheet PDFs (frame metadata) and the documents are local.
- External sources: abstracts of the Elsevier papers listed in the PSI record (Combust. Flame 2017 and 2016, Fire Saf. J. 2016, Proc. Combust. Inst. 35 and 36) are not available through Crossref or Semantic Scholar (publisher-elided), so they were not quoted. Their BASS-II content is reproduced in the TM, except Bhattacharjee et al., Proc. Combust. Inst. 36 (2017) 2963-2969 ('Radiative, thermal, and kinetic regimes...'), whose results are therefore not in this record. NTRS abstracts (20150008961, 20160012691) and the open-access Link et al., Sci. Rep. 8:120 (2018) were used.
- The PSI record's 'removed' list includes a 'BASS-II data FAQ.docx' (legacy, Bhattacharjee folder) that is no longer available.
- Fernandez-Pello: the TM and the 2018 Sci. Rep. paper give different minimum O2 values for opposed spread over rods (TM: 17.5% in microgravity vs 19% in 1 g; Sci. Rep.: 17% vs 18%), and different flow descriptions (TM: spread rate increases with flow; Sci. Rep.: increases then decreases). Both are reported; the record does not resolve which applies.
- Data quality (experimental table CSV, and the same values in TM Appendix A): five 'Calibrated final O2' values are spreadsheet artefacts. B2 1.6, F3 0.9, P22a 0.6 and T28a 0.9 equal the daily sensor offset, and P22b 39.1 and T28b 40.4 equal offset plus raw reading. They were excluded from O2 ranges.
- Data quality (dates): T9 and T18 are dated 6/24/2018 (they belong to 2014, GMT 175; the TM appendix repeats the typo). O10 is dated 7/22/2014 but given GMT 213 (7/22 is GMT 203). Video and still-camera clocks run one day ahead of the actual date because the onboard computer missed the 2014 leap-year handling, so proof-sheet dates (e.g. 'GMT 190 07-09-2014' for test O4, run 7/8/2014) are one day later than the table.
- Data quality (identifiers): sample number '819L' for P24 (others use 'NNNm'); test IDs P7, P11 and T8 are absent; T15 fan display '80122' is two values run together; the 2014 TM total of 126 tests vs 129 table rows is explained by three 2016 Olson runs.
- Data quality (TM text): Table 4.2 lists flow ranges for the rods out of order ('0.043 to 0.02' m/s). Figure 4.4 captions give rod sizes of 4.76 mm and 3.18 mm, which match the radii, not the stated 6.35-12.7 mm diameters. The PMMA formula is printed as C2H8O2 in Section 2.3 (correct: C5H8O2, as in Eq. 1.1). Sample length is given as about 95 mm (Bhattacharjee), 10 cm (Miller, T'ien) and 8.5 cm (Appendix D).
- Data quality (PSI record): co-investigator surname misspelled 'Bhattachjee'; all five contacts are listed as Co-I, while the SRD names Olson as PI and Ferkul as Project Scientist; Olson is not listed among the contacts. The metadata zip is named PSI-101 (SAME) but contains PSI-25 metadata. The record's approach field says flows up to 40 cm/s, while the TM gives about 55 cm/s.
- The exploration-atmosphere comparison uses only the shared reference values; no PSI-25 document gives habitat atmosphere values.
- No credentials or personal data beyond the PSI record's contact e-mails (not reproduced) were found. No text addressed to an AI was found in the documents read.
